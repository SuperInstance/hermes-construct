Dr. Timothy Leary famously proclaimed that "the PC is the LSD of the 1990s".
In his later years, the former Harvard psychologist and 1960s counterculture icon completely pivoted from psychedelic drugs to personal computing, virtual reality, and the early internet. He viewed computers as the ultimate tool for expanding human consciousness and liberating the mind.
To bridge his two eras, Leary even updated his famous '60s catchphrase ("Turn on, tune in, drop out") for the digital age, advising the next generation to:
"Turn on, boot up, jack in."

In his 1983 autobiography Flashbacks, Timothy Leary (https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fm%2F07n39&q=Timothy+Leary) explicitly defined the original 1960s mantra, explaining that it was frequently misinterpreted as an excuse to "get stoned and abandon all constructive activity". The Original Definition (1966)

• "Turn on" meant to go within and activate your dormant neural and genetic equipment. It meant becoming sensitive to different levels of consciousness, using psychedelic drugs as one of many triggers to achieve it. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)
• "Tune in" meant to interact harmoniously with the world around you. It required you to externalize, materialize, and actively express your newly discovered internal perspectives. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)
• "Drop out" meant a graceful, selective process of detachment from unconscious societal commitments. It didn't mean dropping out of life; it meant choosing self-reliance, discovering your own singularity, and committing to mobility, choice, and change. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)

The Digital Evolution (1990s)

Timothy Leary himself changed the phrase to "Turn on, boot up, jack in" during his final decade. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)
As he aged, Leary realized that personal computers, the early internet, and virtual reality were replacing chemical psychedelics as the ultimate tools for cognitive liberty. He used this updated mantra to welcome people to what he called the "cyberdelic counterculture." [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)
At the time, the new terms carried precise, parallel meanings to his original vision:
• "Turn on" remained the command to activate and wake up your own mind.
• "Boot up" meant to power on your personal computer and load the software needed to navigate the digital frontier.
• "Jack in" (a phrase heavily borrowed from 1980s cyberpunk sci-fi like William Gibson's Neuromancer) meant to physically connect your computer to a modem or hook your mind into cyberspace, bypassing traditional, state-controlled mainstream media to form independent, global digital communities. [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_on,_tune_in,_drop_out)

To harness technology instead of being consumed by its simulation, several key shifts must happen:

1. From Attention Architecture to Intention Architecture

• The Problem: Current technology operates as an "extractive television." It treats human attention as a natural resource to be mined, using variable reward loops to keep minds trapped inside corporate world-models.
• The Shift: Platforms must be built on intentionality rather than engagement. Systems must be judged not by how long they keep a user on a screen, but by how efficiently they assist a user in executing a real-world desire before fading entirely into the background.

2. Demanding Open-Source Local Intelligence

• The Problem: Monolithic, cloud-based AI engines act as centralized reality filters. They curate what is true, what is beautiful, and what is possible, turning individuals into passive consumers of a single, synthesized worldview.
• The Shift: Humanity must prioritize local, open-source model execution. Running AI locally on your own hardware means the technology acts as a personalized bicycle for your mind—protecting cognitive sovereignty and keeping your data, thoughts, and cognitive prompts off corporate servers.

3. Cultivating Epistemic Humility in Machines

• The Problem: Current technology frames machine outputs as definitive intelligence, tricking humans into outsourcing their critical thinking, moral reasoning, and creative spark to algorithmic averages.
• The Shift: We must treat technology strictly as a probabilistic mirror, not an oracle. Society must view code as a generator of raw possibilities, while preserving human intuition, contextual awareness, and embodied experience as the only true arbiters of meaning.

4. Designing for "Calm Technology" and Star Trek Ubiquity

• The Problem: The Matrix requires a total plug-in, demanding your eyes, your ears, and your physical paralysis to function.
• The Shift: Developers must adopt the principles of Calm Technology, where computing happens at the periphery of our perception. Like the computer in Star Trek, technology should wait silently until spoken to, execute commands flawlessly without demanding a screen-gaze, and remain completely invisible when not explicitly needed.

5. Intentional Offline Anchoring

• The Problem: When life’s primary rewards (status, validation, wealth) are entirely digitized, humans naturally prioritize the simulation over the physical world.
• The Shift: Communities must consciously re-anchor status and meaning in physical reality.
